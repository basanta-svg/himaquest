Riding the Switchbacks: Bhutan by Motorbike

Notes from the road on the passes, prayer flags and unplanned stops that make touring Bhutan by bike unforgettable.

By Karma Wangchuk • 03 June 2026

There's a particular feeling in leaving Paro on two wheels instead of in the back of a car — the engine settling into a low rumble, the valley opening up on either side, and the knowledge that every bend in the road ahead is now something you'll actually feel rather than watch pass by a window. A Royal Enfield tour through Bhutan turns the drive between destinations into the destination itself.

The Climb Out of Paro

The road out of the Paro valley climbs almost immediately, and within the first hour you're already leaning the bike through switchbacks with terraced fields dropping away below. It's a gentle introduction compared to what comes later, but it's here that you learn to read Bhutan's roads — narrow, well-paved by mountain standards, and never in a straight line for long.

Dochula and Its 108 Chortens

Dochula Pass, at just over 3,100 metres, is where most riders stop for their first real break. The 108 chortens built here in memory of Bhutanese soldiers stand in neat rows against the mountains, and on a clear morning the entire eastern Himalayan range is visible behind them. It's cold enough at this altitude to feel the change from the valley floor, and warm butter tea from one of the roadside stalls is usually the first order of business.

From Dochula the road drops in a long series of hairpins toward Punakha — the kind of descent that rewards a rider who takes it slow and lets the bike do the work.

The Difference Between Riding and Being Driven

Being driven through Bhutan, you see the country. Riding through it, you feel the temperature drop as you climb, smell woodsmoke from a farmhouse before you see it, and hear prayer flags snapping in the wind at a pass before you round the bend. It's a slower, noisier, more physical way to travel — and for riders, that's exactly the point.
