Eleven Passes: What It's Really Like on the Snowman Trek

A day-by-day account of the world's hardest trek, from the last road out of Paro to the high passes above 5,000 metres.

By Karma Wangchuk • 19 May 2026

The Snowman Trek has a reputation that arrives before the trek itself does. Trekkers talk about it in a different register than any other route in the Himalayas — not as a challenge to be conquered, but as something closer to an expedition, one that only a fraction of those who attempt it actually complete. Having guided it several times, I can say the reputation is earned.

Into Lunana

The route runs from Paro through Jomolhari, then continues deep into Lunana — one of the most remote inhabited regions on earth, reachable only on foot. There are no roads here, no way in or out except the same trail you walked in on. Villages are small, scattered and largely self-sufficient, and the sense of distance from anywhere else grows with every day you walk further in.

Eleven Passes Above 4,500 Metres

What sets the Snowman apart from every other trek in Bhutan is simple arithmetic: eleven passes, most above 4,500 metres, several close to 5,000. Altitude here is not a single obstacle to overcome once — it's a daily negotiation, and the trek's length means there's no shortcut around proper acclimatization. Weather at this altitude can turn quickly, and contingency days are built into any serious itinerary for exactly that reason.

This is also why the trek is not attempted lightly. It typically demands three to four weeks, full camping support, and a level of physical readiness closer to a mountaineering expedition than a standard trek.

The Reward at Duer Hot Springs

After days of cold camps and high passes, the natural hot springs at Duer Tshachu feel almost unreal — a place where trekkers traditionally rest for a day or two, soaking sore legs in mineral water piped down from the mountainside. It's one of the trek's few true rest points, and by the time you reach it, you understand exactly why previous generations of travelers built a ritual around it.

Who Should Attempt It

The Snowman Trek is not a first Himalayan trek. It suits experienced high-altitude trekkers who have already tested themselves on shorter routes, who are comfortable with extended time away from any form of resupply, and who can accept that even a well-planned attempt sometimes has to turn back. For those who complete it, it remains one of the defining experiences the Himalayas have to offer.
