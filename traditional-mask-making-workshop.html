Traditional Mask-Making Workshop

About This Experience

The masks used in Bhutan's tshechu dances are far more than costume — each represents a specific deity, protector or demon, and the dance performed while wearing it, the cham, is itself considered a form of meditation and blessing for everyone watching. Carved from light wood, usually alder or willow, and finished in bold reds, golds and blacks, the masks are made by a small number of trained artisans who often learned the craft from a monastery workshop or family lineage.

This workshop puts you alongside one of those artisans to carve and paint a mask of your own, starting with a rough wooden blank and finishing with your own painted design. Along the way you'll learn what the different characters represent — wrathful protector deities, animal-headed guardians, the skeletal Durdag who guard cremation grounds — and why certain colours and expressions are used for certain roles. It's slow, careful work, more about understanding the symbolism than mastering technique in a single sitting, and most guests leave with a genuinely different way of watching a tshechu afterwards.

Sessions run about two to three hours and are best arranged in Thimphu or Paro, where the region's most established mask workshops are based.
